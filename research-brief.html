Ink Extraction Blueprint

Pulling clean handwriting off a messy, low-resolution photo and onto a transparent background. What already exists, how good it actually is, and what you would have to build yourself to ship something lightweight, cross-platform and reliable.

Existing repo to fork?
Partly. The hard vision problem is solved and MIT-licensed. The part you want — soft alpha, RGBA output, an app around it — does not exist.
Best starting point
DocRes (CVPR 2024, MIT, 654★) for the model, DocDiff and NAF-DPM as teachers, not as shipped inference.
Recommended stack
Python core + ONNX, wrapped in Tauri 2 with the ort crate. One model file under 10 MB, no Python on the user's machine.
Off-the-shelf image model?
No. Nano Banana, Seedream and friends redraw strokes rather than extract them, and most cannot emit alpha. But pretrained specialist weights need no fine-tuning either — see §05.
Biggest risk
Generalization, not accuracy. Published models scoring 92 on familiar data collapse to 56–66 on unseen degradation.
01

Why searching for "handwriting extraction" finds nothing

The field you want is not called handwriting extraction. On GitHub that phrase returns a top result with three stars, and every well-populated repo under it is an OCR project — image in, text out. That is a different product.

The academic name is document image binarization, benchmarked by the DIBCO and H-DIBCO competitions from 2009 to 2019, and the broader umbrella is document image restoration. Once you search under those terms you find twenty years of work on exactly your problem: faint ink, uneven lighting, bleed-through from the reverse side, stains, camera noise, low contrast. That literature is the asset here.

The single most useful page on the internet for this project is ZZZHANG-jx/Recommendations-Document-Image-Processing (403★, still updated) — a curated index of every paper, dataset and code release across dewarping, deshadowing, deblurring, binarization and handwritten-text erasure. Start there, not on a search engine.

The honest summary

Nobody has published the thing you described. Every piece is available under a permissive licence; what is missing is the assembly — soft alpha, colour recovery, a capture UI, and a package that runs on three desktop platforms without a Python install. That assembly is your product, and it is a smaller job than it looks.

Read that drop column before you pick a model

Six years of architecture work bought about four points of average F-measure and lost robustness. A transformer at 92.2 average falls to 60.3 on unfamiliar degradation — worse than Sauvola from the year 2000. Your users' photos are all unfamiliar degradation. Optimise the right-hand column, not the left one.

One result worth taking seriously, with a caveat

The 2025 Applied Sciences paper trains a decision-tree ensemble on the outputs of several classical local thresholders plus image statistics, and reports 95.8 across every DIBCO year including 2019 — while running on CPU in low memory. If it replicates, it is the single best fit for "lightweight and reliable" in this entire survey. It is one paper's self-reported cross-validation, so treat it as a strong lead to reproduce early, not as settled fact. Reproducing it is a day of work and would de-risk the whole project.

04

The gap nobody has filled: binary is not transparent

Every model above outputs a 1-bit mask — each pixel is ink or not-ink. If you set alpha = mask and call it a transparent PNG, you get hard, aliased, staircased strokes that look wrong at every zoom level and composite badly onto any coloured background. This is the single biggest reason no academic repo is shippable as-is.

What you actually want is alpha as ink opacity: continuous, antialiased, carrying the natural density falloff at the edge of a pen stroke and the semi-transparency where the pen ran dry. That is a matting problem layered on top of a segmentation problem.

The compositing maths, which is simpler than it sounds

Model each observed pixel as ink laid over paper: I = α·F + (1−α)·B, where B is the local paper colour and F is the pen colour. Estimate B first — a morphological closing with a kernel wider than your thickest stroke, or a rolling-ball background, recovers the paper even under a shadow gradient. Then alpha falls straight out of a division:

# 1. paper background, kernel wider than the thickest stroke B = cv2.morphologyEx(I, cv2.MORPH_CLOSE, ellipse(31)) B = cv2.GaussianBlur(B, (31,31), 0) # smooth the illumination field # 2. divide out the paper -> flat, evenly lit, shadow-free N = clip(I / maximum(B, 1), 0, 1) # 1.0 = bare paper, 0.0 = solid ink # 3. raw opacity, per channel then reduced a = 1.0 - N.min(axis=-1) # min channel keeps coloured ink # 4. gate with the network's PROBABILITY map, never the threshold a = a * smoothstep(p_ink, 0.25, 0.75) # kills paper texture, keeps soft edges # 5. pen colour from the confident core, not the feathered rim F = median(I[erode(p_ink > 0.8, 2)], axis=0) # 6. despeckle: drop components under ~8px at capture resolution a = drop_small_components(a, min_area=8) out = RGBA(F.broadcast_to(I.shape), alpha=a)

Step 4 is the one that matters and the one that is easy to get wrong: gate with the network's probability map, not its thresholded output. Thresholding destroys exactly the sub-pixel information you need for a clean alpha. Every published binarizer throws it away in its final layer; you want the logits.

That is roughly 150 lines of NumPy and OpenCV. It is the piece of genuine novelty in this project, and it will do more for perceived quality than swapping one 92-F-measure model for another.

05

Can a general image model just do this?

A fair question — Nano Banana and Seedream are extraordinary, and nobody wants to train a model they do not have to. The answer turns on a single test, which is worth settling before anything else: does it matter if a stroke changes?

If it does, no current general-purpose image model can do this, for three independent reasons. Any one of them alone would be disqualifying.

They redraw. They do not extract.

The DocRes group built a benchmark for exactly this question — OCRGenBench, 1,060 annotated samples across 33 OCR generative tasks, updated in April 2026 to cover Nano Banana Pro, GPT-Image-1.5 and Seedream-4.5. Their findings on the tasks nearest yours, in their own words:

  • Appearance enhancement — "GPT-4o and OmniGen2 exhibit erroneous text repetition"; Qwen-Image and Janus-4o "generate chaotic, unreadable text".
  • Historical document restoration — "all models fail to preserve original writing style"; they "mostly generate a new document rather than conducting restoration".
  • Deshadowing — most models "mistakenly repeat the textual content".

This is architectural, not a prompting problem

A generative model resynthesizes the image from an understanding of it. For photographs of objects that is a feature. For handwriting it is fatal: the strokes are the product. A model that redraws your handwriting in its own idea of handwriting has destroyed the thing you asked it to keep — and it will do so confidently and legibly, which makes the failure hard to notice at a glance.

The mask route is smarter, and closed

Asking a model for a mask rather than for pixels sidesteps hallucination entirely — the model never touches your ink. It is the right instinct. Both available paths are blocked:

  • Gemini segmentation is deprecated. Google's release notes: "Image segmentation is not supported in Gemini 3.x. For segmentation workloads, continue using Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking off, or Gemini Robotics-ER 1.6." Newer models return polygons rather than per-pixel probability maps, and a polygon cannot describe a pen stroke.
  • SAM 3 does genuine zero-shot text-promptable segmentation (47.0 LVIS mask AP, a 22% jump over the previous best). But it is 3.45 GB and roughly 2.9 s per image on a GPU, and it returns object-level masks. Pen strokes are 2–3 px wide and topologically tangled — the wrong shape of problem, and fatal for a lightweight desktop app.

If you settle for a white background instead of transparency

This changes a great deal — just not in the direction it appears to. It removes the second blocker and leaves the first completely intact. A generative model asked for "clean handwriting on white" will still redraw your strokes; it will simply do so without the checkerboard artefact.

What it does change is that the specialist route becomes trivial. Dark ink on a white ground is the native output format of every model in section 02 — it is literally what binarization means. You can have this today, zero-shot, with no training and no fine-tuning:

python inference.py --im_path ./photo.jpg --task binarization # DocRes, MIT, pretrained

That is the honest answer to "without fine-tuning". Pretrained specialist weights are off-the-shelf — the no-training route was never the language model, it was the model built for this job.

One decision now that keeps both doors open

Output greyscale ink on white, never pure black-and-white binary. Transparency is then one line away — alpha = 1 − grey — and you can ship the white-background version first and add RGBA later without rewriting anything. Keep the estimated pen colour from step 5 of section 04 alongside it and you keep the original blue too. This costs nothing today and saves the whole pipeline later. Throwing away the greyscale is the one irreversible mistake available here.

The strategic cost, which is the real one

"Photo in, clean document on white out" is a solved consumer problem. CamScanner, Adobe Scan, Quark and WPS all do it, they do it well, they are free, and they are already on your users' phones. Transparency is the differentiator — it is what lets someone drop their own handwriting onto a slide, a note-taking app, a design, or a coloured page. Drop it and you have built a worse CamScanner. Keep it and you have built something that does not currently exist.

Where generative models do earn their place

  • As an offline label generator. This is the genuinely good use. Run DocDiff or NAF-DPM across your own captures, hand-correct the output, and train your small U-Net on the result. You distil their quality into 5 MB that runs on a CPU. Phase 1 with a shortcut.
  • As the reasoning layer described in section 09 — looking at a capture and choosing parameters, or resolving "keep only the blue pen" into pipeline settings. Reasoning about the image, never redrawing it.
  • Flux.1-Kontext-dev is worth one experiment if you want to test this yourself: the benchmark found it the strongest on deshadowing, "with most texts preserved and shadow removed", and "nearly perfect" on English document deblurring. Open weights, so you can run it locally.

Five minutes that settle it

Take one messy photo. Ask Nano Banana to remove the background and keep only the handwriting. Then overlay its output on the original at 200% and look at the letterforms. The stroke drift is immediately visible, and it will tell you more than any benchmark table — including this one.

06

The pipeline

Each stage is independently switchable and independently testable. Stages 2, 3 and 9 are optional and should be off by default — every extra model is size, latency and a new failure mode.

01

Capture

Screenshot region, file drop, clipboard paste, or camera. All four land on the same in-memory RGB array; nothing downstream knows the difference.

xcap (Rust) · XDG portal on Wayland · mss (Python fallback)

02

Page detection & perspective correction

Find the sheet, unwarp it. Skip entirely for screenshots and flat scans — it is pure risk there.

OpenCV quad detect, or DocRes dewarping head + MBD

03

Upscale, only if genuinely low-res

Gate on measured stroke width. Under ~2px of stroke there is nothing to recover and a super-resolver will invent plausible ink that was never written.

Real-ESRGAN ×2 · or Lanczos + unsharp for the cheap path

04

Illumination normalization

Estimate the paper background field B. Removes shadows and lighting gradients, and hands stage 7 the value it needs for alpha.

morphological close + Gaussian, kernel > max stroke width

05

Ink probability map

The neural network. Tiled at 512×512 with stride 384 and a Hann-feathered blend. Never resize the whole image — thin strokes disappear and never come back.

U-Net, sigmoid output kept as float, ONNX

06

Handwriting vs. printed split

A second output channel, not a second model. Lets the user keep only the pen and drop the form underneath it — the feature that makes this more than a background remover.

extra head trained on SignaTR6K + EnsExam

07

Alpha compositing & colour recovery

Section 04. Turns a probability map into an RGBA image that survives being dropped on a coloured slide.

~150 lines, NumPy + OpenCV, no model

08

Cleanup & quality gate

Connected-component despeckle. Then check ink-area fraction and contrast against sane bounds; if they are out of range, fall back to the classical path and say so in the UI rather than returning confident nonsense.

cv2.connectedComponentsWithStats

09

Vectorize

The real answer to low resolution: trace once, scale forever. Also what makes the output usable in a note app rather than just viewable.

vtracer (Rust, in-process) or potrace

07

Which model to train, and how big

Do not ship a diffusion model. DocDiff and NAF-DPM produce the best numbers on hard images, but multi-step sampling is flatly incompatible with "lightweight". Use them offline, on your own captures, to generate pseudo-labels — then distil that into one small feed-forward network. You get most of the quality at a fraction of the cost, and your users never wait on a sampler.

Architecture
U-Net with a MobileNetV3-Large or EfficientNet-Lite encoder. Two output heads: ink probability, and handwritten-vs-printed.
Parameters
Target 2–8 M. ConvNeXt-Tiny is reported as the best accuracy/memory trade-off in the ERN-Net study at 28 M — too heavy for a desktop app, useful as your accuracy ceiling during research.
File size
Roughly 10–30 MB at fp32, 3–8 MB at int8. For scale: rembg ships U2Net at 176 MB and U2Net-P at 4 MB, and most users pick the small one.
Input
512×512 tiles, stride 384, dimensions a multiple of 8. Full-resolution sliding window with feathered blending at inference.
Losses
Dice + focal for class imbalance — ink is a few percent of pixels. Add an edge-weighted term so thin strokes are not averaged away.
Export
ONNX opset 17, dynamic batch, static spatial dims. Verify parity against PyTorch on a fixed 50-image set before you trust it.

Training data

This determines reliability far more than architecture does. The public real-image datasets are tiny — all ten DIBCO years together are about 140 images.

  • DIBCO / H-DIBCO 2009–2019 — ~140 images. Your evaluation set, not your training set.
  • LS-HDIB — large-scale handwritten binarization, ICPR 2022. The main real-data source.
  • NoisyOffice (UCI, 216 synthetic) and the shipping-label set (1,082 real).
  • SignaTR6K (6,257), EnsExam (545), Baidu handwriting-erasure (1,281) — these carry handwritten-vs-printed labels and are what make stage 6 possible.
  • Your own captures — 200–500 photos taken the way your users will take them. Worth more than any public set.

The real lever is synthetic composition: take clean ink, composite it over real paper, whiteboard and screenshot backgrounds, then simulate what a phone actually does to it — JPEG blocking, downscale-then-upscale, motion blur, illumination gradients, cast shadows, bleed-through, sensor noise, moiré, perspective. The ERN-Net paper reports DIBCO-style synthetic pretraining is worth +2.2 F-measure at zero inference cost, for about 90 minutes of extra training on a mid-range GPU.

08

Shipping it: lightweight and cross-platform

Two layers. A Python core that is the reference implementation and the CLI, and a Rust/Tauri shell that is what users install. The shell never needs a Python runtime on the user's machine.

LayerChoiceWhy
Core libraryPython · onnxruntime · opencv · numpyWhere you develop, benchmark and train. Ships as a pip package and a CLI for the technical users.
Desktop shellTauri 2~15 MB installer against Electron's 100 MB+. System webview, Rust backend, real global shortcuts and tray.
Inferenceort crate (ONNX Runtime)One measured migration reports 32.7 ms → 18 ms moving from browser WASM to native ort, with lower memory variance.
AccelerationCoreML / DirectML / CUDA / CPURegistered per-target at build time. Always keep a CPU path — it is the one that never fails.
Screen capturexcapWindows, macOS and Linux from one API. On macOS you must request Screen Recording (TCC) permission.

Two packaging traps found in the research

ONNX Runtime and AppImage. Building ort with the load-dynamic feature produces a Linux bundle that fails at runtime with cannot open shared object file: libonnxruntime.so, because the bundler cannot see a dependency resolved at load time. Either use the download-binaries feature or add an explicit fetch-and-bundle step. Real projects have shipped broken releases over this.

Wayland screenshots. Raw X11 grabs return a black frame under Wayland. You need the XDG desktop portal, org.freedesktop.portal.Screenshot, which is a different code path with a permission prompt. Budget for it — Linux users will hit it on day one, and Python's mss does not solve it.

09

On calling it an agent

You described this as an AI agent. Worth being precise, because it changes what you build: the pixel path here should be a deterministic vision pipeline, and keeping it that way is what makes it reliable. Generative models hallucinate strokes — the DocRes authors published a 2025 evaluation specifically on this failure mode, and it is not subtle. A model that invents a plausible letter where the ink had faded has destroyed the document.

There is real room for a language model, just not in the pixel path:

  • Parameter selection — look at the capture, decide whether to dewarp, upscale, or drop straight to the classical path.
  • Natural-language control — "keep only the blue pen", "thicken the strokes", "drop the printed form" resolved into pipeline parameters.
  • Post-processing — naming, filing, and OCR'ing the extracted ink into searchable text alongside the PNG.
  • Triage — explaining why a capture came out badly, which is the difference between a tool people trust and one they abandon.

Build the pipeline first and make it excellent. The agent layer is a thin wrapper over a well-specified parameter surface, and it is much easier to add to a deterministic core than to retrofit determinism into a generative one.

10

What will go wrong

  • Generalization, not accuracy. Section 03's drop column is the whole story. Build a golden set of your own real captures on day one, score with the DIBCO metrics, and never optimise against DIBCO alone.
  • Sub-2-pixel strokes cannot be recovered. Below roughly 100 DPI the information is gone. Super-resolution will produce something convincing and wrong. Detect this case and tell the user plainly.
  • Pencil and coloured ink. Graphite on textured paper has almost no contrast against the texture; highlighter is lighter than the paper in some channels. Both break the assumption baked into every DIBCO-trained model. Collect these cases early.
  • Weight licensing. DocEnTr carries no clear licence and GDB carries none at all. You cannot redistribute those weights. DocRes, DocDiff and NAF-DPM are MIT — check the weight files separately from the code, since DocRes hosts its checkpoints off-repo.
  • Tiling seams. Visible as faint vertical or horizontal lines through strokes. Fixed by feathered blending, not by a larger tile.
  • Bleed-through. Ink from the reverse side is genuinely ambiguous — it is real ink. Six of the twenty DIBCO 2019 images have it, which is a large part of why that benchmark breaks models.
11

Sequenced build

PHASE 0 · ~1 week

Classical baseline

Background division, Sauvola, connected-component filtering, alpha compositing from section 04. No model at all. Assemble the golden test set of 200+ real captures in the same week.

Ships. Becomes the permanent fallback path.
PHASE 1 · ~2 weeks

Learned ink mask

Small U-Net on DIBCO + LS-HDIB + heavy synthetic augmentation. Export to ONNX. Reproduce the 2025 ensemble result in parallel and keep whichever wins on your golden set.

Measurable win over Phase 0, or it does not ship.
PHASE 2 · ~2 weeks

The app

Tauri shell, global-shortcut region capture, drag-drop, live preview on a checkerboard, PNG and SVG export. Quality gate wired to the fallback.

The product a non-technical user can install.
PHASE 3 · open-ended

Sharpen

Handwritten-vs-printed head. Diffusion distillation on your own captures. Optional dewarp and upscale. Natural-language controls. Browser and mobile builds.

Driven by what the golden set still fails.
